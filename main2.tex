\begin{abstract}
Financial markets exhibit distinct regimes characterized by varying levels of volatility, momentum, and risk appetite. Identifying these regimes is crucial for robust quantitative trading strategies, particularly those based on intraday momentum. In this report, we present a comprehensive pipeline for market regime detection utilizing a multi-start Gaussian Hidden Markov Model (HMM) fitted on macro-financial indicators. We outline our data collection and cleaning process, dimensionality reduction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and the HMM training framework incorporating a penalized Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) for model selection. Furthermore, we contrast the HMM approach—which models temporal state persistence—with a Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) hierarchical clustering algorithm. Our results demonstrate that incorporating temporal awareness significantly improves the identification of structural volatility regimes over static clustering methods, yielding actionable insights for dynamic portfolio risk management.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Motivation and Context}
Implementing robust trading strategies, such as an intraday momentum strategy for the S\&P 500 (SPY), requires the ability to identify distinct market regimes (e.g., Bull, Bear, or Sideways markets). By detecting the prevailing regime, quantitative models can dynamically adjust trading parameters—such as sizing, stop-losses, and entry thresholds—to match current market conditions.

A commonly used statistical framework for identifying such regimes is the Hidden Markov Model (HMM), which models financial returns as emissions from latent states governed by a Markov process. In empirical finance, Gaussian HMMs have been widely applied to detect persistent low-volatility and high-volatility regimes in major equity indices such as the S\&P 500. These models frequently associate high-volatility states with periods of market stress, including events like the Global Financial Crisis and the COVID-19 market crash. Their ability to capture regime persistence while remaining relatively interpretable makes HMMs a practical tool for volatility regime detection and for informing adaptive trading and risk-management frameworks.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Financial markets are intrinsically noisy, and standard indicators often suffer from high multicollinearity and a lack of temporal awareness. Conventional models that rely solely on static thresholds or simple heuristics tend to overfit to momentary noise, generating false signals during transient market shocks. There is a critical need for a probabilistic model capable of capturing persistent regimes across price action, volatility, macroeconomic shifts, and credit indicators, while effectively filtering out isolated noise.

\subsection{Project Scope and Methodology}
To address these challenges, we have developed a data-driven regime detection pipeline. Our methodology involves gathering broad market and macroeconomic data, cleaning and standardizing the dataset, and performing dimensionality reduction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to orthogonalize the features. We then fit a multi-start Gaussian Hidden Markov Model (HMM) on these principal components. The optimal number of hidden states is determined using a penalized Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) that penalizes models with unrealistically short-lived regimes. To benchmark and contextualize our findings, we also explore a Hierarchical Clustering algorithm incorporating Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance over rolling windows. This report details the data pipeline, feature engineering, model selection, and provides a comparative analysis of the clustering and HMM methodologies.

\section{Literature review}
% ===================================================================

Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) are widely employed to detect latent volatility regimes in financial time series. In the standard Gaussian specification, log-returns are modeled as state-dependent Gaussian emissions governed by a finite-state Markov chain, with parameters estimated via the Baum--Welch Expectation-Maximization algorithm \cite{Rabiner1989, Holzmann2016}. Most studies adopt two or three regimes, balancing interpretability and parsimony, and rely on information criteria or out-of-sample performance to determine the appropriate model order \cite{Chen2020}.

Empirical work consistently finds that Gaussian HMMs identify persistent low-volatility ``bull'' regimes and episodic high-volatility ``crisis'' regimes. Applications to the S\&P~500, CSI~300, EuroStoxx, and major futures typically show that the highest-volatility state coincides with well-known market drawdowns such as the 2008 financial crisis or the 2020 COVID shock \cite{Wang2017, Duriez2025, Liu2017}. Regime-conditioned trading strategies often display improved drawdown control and modestly enhanced Sharpe ratios relative to buy-and-hold or simple technical-rule benchmarks \cite{Wang2020, Duriez2025}. Studies comparing HMMs with moving-average filters or unsupervised clustering generally conclude that the Markovian persistence embedded in HMMs produces more stable and economically interpretable regime assignments \cite{Ansari2025, LSEG2023}.

Despite their practicality, Gaussian HMMs are subject to well-known limitations. The Gaussian assumption understates fat tails, and the time-homogeneous transition matrix restricts the model's ability to capture structural breaks or long-memory volatility \cite{Ansari2025}. Regime estimates are also sensitive to initialization and to the inclusion or exclusion of outliers. Feature sets frequently rely on raw or log-returns, and empirical evidence on multivariate or indicator-augmented HMMs is mixed, with improvements depending heavily on sample period and preprocessing \cite{Chen2020, QuantConnect2023}.

Recent research highlights the importance of rigorous out-of-sample validation. Rolling or expanding-window HMMs generally outperform full-sample fits by accommodating slow parameter drift, yet challenges remain regarding real-time regime detection and the prevention of look-ahead biases in performance evaluation \cite{Duriez2025, Ansari2025}. Extensions incorporating heavy-tailed emissions, time-varying transitions, or higher-dimensional feature sets represent active areas for further development \cite{Wang2020, LSEG2023}.

Overall, the literature establishes Gaussian HMMs as a robust and interpretable framework for volatility regime identification. They deliver consistent qualitative insights and offer incremental improvements in risk-adjusted returns, though their effectiveness depends on careful model specification, data handling, and out-of-sample validation procedures \cite{Duriez2025, Wang2020}.

\section{Data Collection Pipeline}
% ===================================================================
Our unified \texttt{DataManager} component orchestrates the retrieval and alignment of all necessary datasets.

\subsection{Data Retrieval}
We acquire approximately 3 years (circa 800 trading days) of financial and macroeconomic data from established sources:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Market Data}: Intraday and daily data for the S\&P 500 ETF (SPY), the VIX volatility index, the MOVE bond volatility index, and the long-term Treasury ETF (TLT) are fetched via Yahoo Finance.
    \item \textbf{Macroeconomic Data}: Interest rates and credit metrics, including the 10-Year and 2-Year Treasury yields (\texttt{DGS10}, \texttt{DGS2}) and Corporate Bond yields (\texttt{AAA}, \texttt{BAA}), are retrieved via the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) API.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cleaning and Merging}
Due to the disparate sampling frequencies and holiday schedules across equity and bond markets, rigorous data cleaning is applied. The pipeline forward-fills missing values to address market closures, verifies business-day continuity, and removes spurious anomalies. Intraday equity data is aggregated into standard daily OHLCV formats. Finally, all datasets are merged via an "as-of" join strategy, aligning them strictly to a common daily trading calendar to form the master dataset.

\subsection{Feature Computation and Dimensionality Reduction}
From the raw clean data, we compute several derived features, including the Credit Spread (BAA - AAA) and the Yield Curve slope (10Y - 2Y Treasury yields).

To address multicollinearity, which is prevalent among financial indicators, we compute Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). Instead of iteratively dropping collinear variables and potentially losing information, we group features into semantic categories (\texttt{market}, \texttt{credit}, and \texttt{cross\_asset}) and apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) independently to each. We retain a sufficient number of principal components to explain strictly 80\% of the variance within each category.

\section{Validation Summary}
% ===================================================================

This section documents the validation process implemented by Team 1, referencing the integrity report \cite{team1_report}.

\subsection{Validation Process}
Prior to analysis, a series of preprocessing steps was performed to ensure data consistency and temporal alignment \cite{team1_script}. First, the corporate bond variables (\texttt{AAA}, \texttt{BAA}, and the \texttt{credit\_spread\_baa\_aaa}) were rescaled from percentage units to decimal form. Second, missing observations in the Treasury yield series (\texttt{DGS2}, \texttt{DGS10}) and the \texttt{yield\_curve\_10y\_2y\_} spread were imputed using a forward-filling technique, in addition to converting their values from percentage units to decimal form.

Similarly, the \texttt{MOVE} and \texttt{TLT} datasets underwent a limited preprocessing protocol. Specifically, the forward-filling technique was exclusively applied to address missing dates and ensure time-series continuity; no further transformations or rescaling were performed on these variables.

The \texttt{SPY} dataset underwent daily aggregation to extract the minimum, maximum, open, and close prices. Gaps in trading days were identified and forward-filled; the specific nature of these missing dates is discussed in Section \ref{sec:integrity_analysis}. A similar imputation method was applied to the \texttt{VIX} index without aggregation. Finally, to address the temporal discrepancy between the two-year span of the \texttt{SPY} data and the one-year span of the remaining variables, the datasets were synchronized, restricting the final sample to the most recent year of intersecting data.

\subsection{Integrity Report Analysis} \label{sec:integrity_analysis}
Temporal gaps in the datasets, primarily attributable to market closures and U.S. statutory holidays, were addressed by assuming a continuous time series across all business days. Consequently, missing values for these non-trading dates were imputed using the forward-filling technique \cite{team1_validation}.

Furthermore, a data inconsistency was identified and resolved regarding the observation on January 9, 2025. While the \texttt{SPY} and \texttt{VIX} datasets correctly reflected a market closure, the \texttt{yield\_curve\_10y\_2y\_spread} series contained a spurious non-null value. To ensure consistency across all variables, this anomalous data point was removed and substituted using the forward-filling method.

\section{Engineered Features}
% ===================================================================

This section introduces a new set of macro–financial features that will be used downstream in the Market Regime Detector.

\subsection{Inputs and Output}
The feature construction is implemented in \texttt{src/classes/metrics/curve\_credit.py} and relies on the following cleaned inputs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{data/cleaned/dgs10.csv}: 10-year Treasury yield (\texttt{DGS10}).
    \item \texttt{data/cleaned/dgs2.csv}: 2-year Treasury yield (\texttt{DGS2}).
    \item \texttt{data/cleaned/credit\_spread.csv}: BAA–AAA corporate bond spread.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Yield Curve Slope Feature}
The first feature captures the slope of the US Treasury yield curve between 10-year and 2-year maturities. Starting from the cleaned series \texttt{DGS10} and \texttt{DGS2}, the script computes the difference in decimal units:
$ \texttt{curve\_10y\_2y}_t = \texttt{DGS10}_t - \texttt{DGS2}_t $.

\subsection{Credit Spread Feature}
The second feature encodes corporate credit risk premia via the BAA–AAA spread, dynamically forward-filling missing values along the credit spread time index.

\subsection{Macro Fill Policy and Daily Alignment}
Because the yield curve features are available at (near) daily frequency, whereas credit spreads may be reported at lower frequency, the two series are aligned using a macro fill policy implemented via an as-of merge using \texttt{pandas.merge\_asof} with \texttt{direction="backward"}.

\subsection{Market-Based Features (SPX)}
Following strict statistical requirements for the Gaussian HMM model, we restrict the equity feature space to three Gaussian-compatible variables to avoid multicollinearity. We compute:

\begin{description}
    \item[R1: Daily Return] $ r_{t,1d} = \ln(P_t) - \ln(P_{t-1}) $
    \item[R2: 5-Day Return] $ r_{t,5d} = \ln(P_t) - \ln(P_{t-5}) $
    \item[V1': Log-Transformed Realized Volatility (14d)] 
    To satisfy the Gaussian emission assumption, the 14-day annualized realized volatility is log-transformed:
    $ V1' = \ln \left( \sqrt{ 252 \cdot \frac{1}{14} \sum_{i=1}^{14} r_{t-i,1d}^{2} } \right) $
\end{description}

Finally, stationarity across these features is formally verified via Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and KPSS statistical tests.

\textit{(Sprint Update)}: Moving beyond preliminary heuristics, we explicitly cross-validate the probabilistic outputs (HMM and DTW clusters) against financial realities. We define:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bull Regime:} Characterized by positive average returns ($R1, R2 > 0$), low realized volatility ($V1'$), and minimal drawdowns. Coherent with upward trends and tightening credit.

\end{itemize}
This cross-feature validation ensures that the abstract mathematical states correspond to genuine macroeconomic shifts.

\subsection{HMM Training and Selection}
The processed, orthogonalized PCA features are fed into a multi-start Gaussian Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with diagonal covariance, implemented via \texttt{hmmlearn}. We fit the HMM exploring 2 to 4 hidden states, extracting the state sequence, means, and variances. Because HMM optimization is highly non-convex, the pipeline trains dozens of models with different random seeds.

To determine the optimal number of hidden regimes ($k$), we employ a custom penalized Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Standard BIC often overestimates $k$ in financial time series; our penalized BIC introduces a severe complexity penalty if the expected duration of any inferred state falls below a minimum threshold (e.g., 5 days), forcing the model to return a structurally sound set of regimes.

\subsection{Clustering Visualization and Interpretation}
Once the optimal HMM is selected, we perform inference via the Viterbi algorithm. Semantic interpretation is strictly data-driven: labels are automatically assigned by sorting the Gaussian means across the primary PCA axis, which typically explains the bulk of market variance.

\section{HMM vs. Clustering}
% ===================================================================

\subsection{Static vs. Temporal Clustering}
Classical distance-based clustering algorithms, such as K-Means, operate purely in the spatial dimension. In volatile financial markets, this often leads to "salt-and-pepper" classification, where the label rapidly switches back and forth between regimes on a daily basis.

To combat this, our alternative approach segments the time series into rolling windows of \textbf{21 trading days}. We explicitly assume this duration because 21 days roughly correspond to one standard trading month—an ideal window to capture persistent macroeconomic regimes while filtering out transient daily noise. We compute the Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance between these multidimensional windows, and hierarchical clustering is applied to the distance matrix.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]

\subsection{The Benefit of Markovian Persistence}
While DTW clustering represents an improvement over static K-Means, the Hidden Markov Model provides a more elegant probabilistic framework for capturing volatility clustering. Financial volatility natively exhibits strong auto-correlation. 

An HMM natively captures this temporal persistence through its transition probability matrix. Ultimately, the Markovian framework creates clean, persistent boundaries between high-volatility "Bear/Risk-Off" regimes and low-volatility sustained "Bull" markets, delivering highly reliable and actionable signals for dynamic strategy allocation.
